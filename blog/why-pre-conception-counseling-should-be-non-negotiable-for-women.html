Why Pre-Conception Counseling Should Be Non-Negotiable for Women

By Dr. Sai Lakshmikanth Bharathi, MD · September 7, 2026

A few days ago, a 33-year-old woman with diabetes walked into my clinic — not because of a complication, not for a routine follow-up, but because she wants to have a baby. She came before conceiving, not after finding out she was pregnant. That single decision — to seek advice first — may end up being one of the most important choices she makes for her child's health.


It's a scene I wish played out far more often. In my years of practice, I've seen the difference between women who plan their pregnancy around their diabetes and those who find out about diabetes control only after a pregnancy test turns positive. The difference in outcomes is not subtle — it can mean the difference between a healthy full-term baby and a pregnancy marked by miscarriage, congenital malformation, or a difficult NICU stay.

Here's what most patients don't realize: a baby's heart, brain, spine, and major organs form in the first 6–8 weeks of pregnancy — often before a woman even knows she's pregnant. If blood sugars are poorly controlled during this window, the risk of congenital malformations, miscarriage, and stillbirth rises sharply. By the time a woman with irregular cycles or unplanned pregnancy realizes she's expecting, that critical window may have already passed.

This is precisely why pre-conception counseling exists — to move the "diabetes conversation" from the second trimester back to months before conception, when we can actually influence those outcomes.

For my patients planning a pregnancy, counseling isn't a single conversation — it's a structured plan:

  • Tightening glycemic control first. We aim for an HbA1c as close to 6.5% as safely possible before stopping contraception, without tipping into frequent hypoglycemia. If HbA1c is very high, I'm honest with patients that conception should wait until levels improve.
  • Reviewing every medication. Many oral anti-diabetic drugs and other medications (certain blood pressure medicines, statins, some others) aren't safe in pregnancy and need to be switched — often to insulin — before conception, not after a positive pregnancy test.
  • Screening for complications. Diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, thyroid dysfunction, and hypertension all need to be assessed and, where possible, stabilized before pregnancy, since pregnancy itself can accelerate some of these.
  • Starting folic acid early, along with individualized advice on weight, diet, and exercise.
  • Contraception until ready. Effective contraception is recommended until glycemic control and medication changes are actually in place — pregnancy should be a planned event, not a surprise superimposed on uncontrolled sugars.
  • Setting expectations. Pregnancy in diabetes requires closer monitoring, more frequent visits, and active self-monitoring of blood glucose. Patients do better when they know this going in.

This conversation matters just as much for women at risk of gestational diabetes (GDM) — those with a family history of diabetes, PCOS, obesity, prior GDM, or older maternal age. Identifying this risk before conception allows for lifestyle modification and early screening strategies, rather than a late diagnosis in the second trimester after fetal growth has already been affected.

In my experience, the biggest obstacle isn't clinical complexity; it's that most women don't know pre-conception counseling exists as a service, or assume "I'll deal with it once I'm pregnant." Many diabetic pregnancies are still unplanned, which means the single most powerful window for prevention is missed entirely.
This is where we, as physicians, need to be proactive — raising this conversation with every woman of reproductive age who has diabetes or risk factors for it, at every visit, not waiting for her to ask.

If you have diabetes and are even considering pregnancy in the next year, please come in for a conversation before you start trying. It costs you one visit and a few tests. What it can protect against is immeasurable.

And to my fellow physicians: let's normalize asking "Are you planning a pregnancy?" as routinely as we ask about diet and exercise. It's a simple question that can change the entire trajectory of a pregnancy.
